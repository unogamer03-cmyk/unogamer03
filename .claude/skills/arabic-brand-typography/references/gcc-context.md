# Saudi and GCC Context for Arabic Brand Typography

Regulations and official guidance change. Treat the legal and regulatory points below as **things to verify**, not as legal advice. Point the user to the relevant authority and to their local legal counsel or trademark agent.

## Arabic prominence on signage (KSA)

Saudi municipal signage rules require the Arabic trade name on storefront signs. A foreign-language name may also appear, but rules have generally required the Arabic to be at least as prominent, often with Arabic placed first (to the right or above). Check the current requirements of the Ministry of Municipalities and Housing and the Balady platform for the specific city and sign type before finalizing signage lockups.

Design implication: every Saudi brand used on a storefront needs a signage-ready bilingual lockup where the Arabic leads or is equal. Design this from day one. Don't wait until the signage vendor asks.

## Trademarks and protected symbols

- Trademark registration in KSA is handled by **SAIP** (Saudi Authority for Intellectual Property). Marks containing national emblems, the flag, religious symbols or expressions, or marks offensive to public order are generally refused. Have the user confirm with a trademark agent.
- The Saudi flag carries the Shahada. It must not be used decoratively, on disposable items, or in commercial branding without authorization. The national emblem (palm and swords) is likewise protected.
- Avoid the name of Allah, Qur'anic verses, and the Shahada on products, packaging, floor graphics, or anything likely to be discarded or stepped on.

## Language register

- **فصحى (MSA)**: the default for institutional, government, financial, healthcare, and education brands. It conveys formality and trust.
- **Saudi dialect** (Najdi, Hijazi, and others): effective for youth, F&B, retail, and social campaigns. It feels local and warm. Make sure the dialect matches the audience's region, and keep legal or terms copy in MSA.
- Transliterated English names: agree on one Arabic spelling and lock it in the guidelines (for example, whether to use ڤ/ﭪ for "v" or plain ف, and whether to add long vowels). Inconsistent spelling across touchpoints is a common brand failure.
- For copy quality (avoiding translationese, Arabic pluralization), the `arabic-ui` skill complements this one.

## Numerals

- **Western digits (0–9)** are widely used in Saudi digital products, prices, and many corporate identities.
- **Arabic-Indic digits (٠–٩)** are used in traditional, governmental, editorial, and heritage contexts.
- Decide per brand and document it: phone numbers, prices, dates, data visualization.
- Avoid **Persian/Eastern Arabic-Indic digits (۴ ۵ ۶)** in Gulf work.
- Check that the chosen Arabic font's numerals match the Latin numerals' style (lining vs old-style, proportional vs tabular for tables).

## Dates and calendar

Many Saudi contexts show both Gregorian and Hijri dates. Brand moments that often need dedicated typographic treatment:
- **Ramadan and Eid** (dates move on the Gregorian calendar each year).
- **Saudi Founding Day**: 22 February.
- **Saudi National Day**: 23 September.
- Riyadh Season and other city or seasonal events.

For national occasions, the official identity guidelines published for that year usually govern logo use. Don't modify or imitate official emblems.

## Visual clichés to avoid (unless justified and specific)

- Generic "Arabian" icons: lanterns, crescents, domes, camels, palm silhouettes, dunes used as decoration.
- Gold-on-black "luxury Arabic" by default.
- Reflexive calligraphic flourishes on every brand.
- Copying the current government-digital visual language (for example, a Vision 2030-style look) for a private brand. It reads as borrowed authority.

Stronger alternatives: draw from **specific** local references, such as Najdi architecture (mud-brick triangular openings), Sadu weaving patterns, Hijazi rawasheen woodwork, Al-Ula's landscape and inscriptions, or Asir's Al-Qatt painting. Do this with research and respect, attributing the craft context where appropriate.

## Bilingual reading behavior

- Saudi readers switch scripts constantly. A layout that forces the eye to zig-zag (Arabic right-aligned, Latin left-aligned in alternating lines) tires them. Prefer separate columns or blocks per language, or a clear primary language with secondary support.
- In RTL layouts, mirror the layout direction (navigation, arrows, progress indicators), but don't mirror logos, Latin text, media controls, or clocks.
